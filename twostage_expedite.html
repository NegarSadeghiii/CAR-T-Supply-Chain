Parameter Guide

Parameter Value used What it means Effect of increasing
σ_L (sigma_L) 0.2 Spread of manufacturing time T_MF. T_MF ~ LogNormal with this log-scale std deviation. σ_L = 0 means T_MF is always exactly 7 days (deterministic). σ_L = 0.20 means roughly ±20% variation around the mean. More uncertainty → higher P(expedite) → higher expected cost premium over deterministic.
δ (delta) 2.0d Days saved by triggering expedited return shipping. When T_MF > K_i (slack), expediting cuts return transport time by δ days, allowing the patient to still meet their deadline. Larger δ → more routes become feasible under recourse → more plans available → lower cost.
ε (epsilon) 0.05 Plan filter strictness. Only routes where expediting can still rescue the deadline with probability ≥ 1−ε are kept. ε = 0.05 means: even in the worst 5% of T_MF draws, the route must be recoverable. Lower ε → stricter filter → fewer plans → higher cost or infeasibility.
τ (tau) 0.0 Deadline tightness factor (0 to 1). Shrinks patient deadlines to stress-test the model. τ = 0 uses original deadlines (easiest). τ = 1 tightens deadlines to the minimum possible. Higher τ → tighter deadlines → fewer feasible routes → higher cost or infeasibility.
π (pi) H:$8k M:$4k L:$1.5k Cost of triggering expedited return for a High / Medium / Low urgency patient. This enters the objective as E[$exp] = π_p ⋅ P(T_MF > K_i) per patient. Higher π → expediting is more expensive → solver avoids tight routes more aggressively.

Model Design

Why LogNormal for T_MF? We model the manufacturing time T_MF as LogNormal(μ_L, σ_L=0.2). Biological processes are always positive, typically close to their mean, but occasionally run much longer — a skewed, right-tailed shape that LogNormal captures naturally. The parameter σ_L controls the spread: at 0.20, you get roughly ±20 % variation around the 7-day nominal. Mean is preserved via μ_L = ln(T̄_MF) − σ_L²/2.
CCP (Chance-Constrained Programming) takes a conservative, single-stage approach. Instead of planning with the nominal 7-day T_MF, it inflates the manufacturing budget to T̂_MF = F⁻¹(1−α) — long enough that the plan holds in 90 % of scenarios. Every route decision is locked in before manufacturing starts. If T_MF runs over the inflated budget, there is no backup: the deadline is simply missed.
Two-Stage Expediting ColGen is more flexible. Routes are still chosen before manufacturing, but now there is a recourse action: if T_MF exceeds K_i — the maximum overtime the route can absorb — expedited return shipping is triggered, saving δ=2.0d on the return leg at cost π_p per patient. A violation only occurs when T_MF > K_i + δ (even expediting cannot save it). The model prices this risk directly into the column cost: c̃_i = c_transport + π_p ⋅ P(T_MF > K_i), so routes with high expediting probability are penalised at selection time. ε=0.05 is a hard filter: any route where even expediting leaves > 5 % chance of a violation is discarded entirely.
Key difference: CCP commits upfront to a costly conservative plan and cannot adapt. Two-Stage carries a small expected-cost premium for the ability to react when manufacturing runs late. The out-of-sample tab quantifies the cost – reliability trade-off.

What This Work Is About

We built two smarter versions of a scheduling model for CAR-T cell therapy. The main challenge is that the time it takes to manufacture the cells for a patient is not fixed — it varies. We modelled this variability using a LogNormal distribution, which means manufacturing time is always positive, usually close to 7 days, but occasionally runs much longer. We chose LogNormal because biological processes tend to behave this way — they are skewed, not symmetric. The parameter σ_L controls how spread out that variability is. At 0.20, you are looking at roughly ±20 % around the average.

CCP versus Two-Stage
The first model, CCP, takes a conservative approach. Instead of planning with the average 7-day manufacturing time, it assumes a longer worst-case value upfront — long enough that the plan will still work 90 % of the time. Everything is decided before manufacturing starts, and if things go wrong there is nothing to do: the plan either holds or it does not.

The second model, Two-Stage Expediting, is more flexible. You still pick the route before manufacturing starts, but now you have a backup plan. If manufacturing runs too long and the patient’s deadline is at risk, you pay for expedited return shipping — saving δ days on the return leg. The question is: how much overtime can this particular route absorb before you need to call in that backup? That is what K_i represents — the maximum manufacturing time the route can handle before things start breaking down. If manufacturing exceeds K_i, you expedite. If it exceeds K_i plus δ, even expediting will not be enough. The model prices this risk directly into the objective, so routes with a high chance of needing expediting are penalised in the cost. ε is a safety filter that discards any route where even with expediting there is still more than a 5 % chance of missing the deadline.
Out-of-sample comparison
To compare the two models fairly, we ran both solutions through 10 000 fresh scenarios they had never seen — asking: how does each hold up in the real world? Each patient receives their own independent manufacturing draw per scenario, because CAR-T batches are patient-specific. For CCP, since there is no backup action, any scenario where manufacturing runs past the planned limit causes a deadline violation. For Two-Stage, the expediting option absorbs most of those cases. What we found is that CCP violates deadlines in around 8–9 % of scenarios, while Two-Stage keeps violations down to around 3 %. The trade-off is that Two-Stage costs slightly more on average because it pre-prices the expediting risk into every route. So it is really a choice between a cheaper but more fragile plan, and a slightly more expensive but much more resilient one.
Violation superiority of Two-Stage
The gap between 8–9 % (CCP) and 3 % (Two-Stage) violations is not just a statistical artefact — it reflects a structural difference in how the two models handle risk. CCP bets everything on its inflated budget being enough; the moment T_MF exceeds that single threshold, the patient’s deadline is broken with no recovery. Two-Stage, by contrast, has a second line of defence: once T_MF crosses K_i, expediting is activated. A hard violation only occurs when manufacturing is so late that even expediting — which saves δ=2.0 days — cannot recover the deadline. This layered structure compresses the “failure region” from one threshold to two, which is why Two-Stage violations are roughly 3× lower than CCP despite having a similar or lower transport cost. As N grows and more patients are scheduled, this reliability advantage compounds: the probability that at least one patient misses a deadline is much lower under Two-Stage than under CCP (see the violation-vs-N chart in the OOS tab).

Manufacturing Cost Impact — What the Model Actually Controls

What changes with scheduling: The model selects which manufacturing facility each patient uses and when their batch starts. This affects three cost categories:
  • Facility fixed cost — (CIM + CVM) × T_max is charged for every open facility regardless of load. Concentrating patients into one facility avoids opening a second at $3M+ fixed cost. This is the dominant lever: facility cost represents 85–90% of total cost at most N values.
  • Transportation cost — Route selection (j1 vs j2, facility location) drives outbound + return costs. This is small (<2% of total) but the only cost dimension that differs between j1 and j2 routes.
  • Material cost — Fixed at $(10,476 + 9,312) × N. Identical across all models. The schedule does not change material consumption.
What the model does NOT capture — rework cost: In CAR-T manufacturing, a batch that fails QC must be reworked or restarted, adding significant cost (often $50,000–$200,000 per failed batch). Manufacturing delays (T_MF > TMFE) may correlate with higher rework probability. If schedule tightness increases rework risk, then Two-Stage’s conservative route selection (K_i filter, capacity buffered at 90th-pct) may indirectly reduce rework costs beyond what is captured in the objective. Quantifying this link — e.g., via a rework probability function of schedule slack — is a natural extension and would strengthen the case for stochastic scheduling in CAR-T supply chains.
Advisor note: The current cost function is transport-and-facility dominant. The model does influence which facility opens (the most consequential decision), which transport mode is used, and implicitly how much slack a patient has against their deadline. If rework or batch failure costs were added to the objective as a function of schedule tightness, the manufacturing-cost impact would become direct and measurable.

Value of the Stochastic Solution (VSS)

VSS = EEV − RP, where:
• RP (Recourse Problem) = Two-Stage optimal cost (route selection with expediting recourse priced in)
